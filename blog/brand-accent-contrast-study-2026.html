37.1% of the brand accents we measured fail WCAG AA with white text

By Yao Xiangqian · 2026-11-11 · 6 min read · data: CSV

We pulled the dominant accent colour out of the CSS on 70 well-known homepages and checked it against WCAG 2.2. With white text — the default label colour on most brand buttons — 37.1% of accents (26 of 70) fail the 4.5:1 requirement, and 14 fail even the 3:1 large-text threshold. With black text, the picture inverts almost completely: 0 accents fail both. The colour is rarely the problem; the label colour is.

Why the label colour matters more than the brand colour

Contrast is symmetric but not symmetric in practice: designers pick white labels on brand buttons far more often than black, because white reads as "clean" on a saturated surface. The measurement says that instinct takes a mid-tone accent and drops it below the normal-text threshold.

Take a mid blue at #2560FF: 5.00:1 with white, which passes. Now the same hue one step lighter, #3B82F6: 3.68:1 — still fine to the eye, and below 4.5:1 on paper. The colour did not get worse; the pairing did.

Yellow, green and cyan accents are close to unusable as a white-text surface — the median yellow in this sample scores 1.26:1, which is effectively invisible. Blue is the only band where a majority of accents can carry white text, which explains why it stays the default accent of the web. The practical rule: the lighter or more saturated toward yellow the accent, the more likely the label should be near-black.

If your accent is in the failing band

  1. Try the label colour first. Switch white to #111827. It costs nothing, keeps the brand colour, and clears 4.5:1 for most mid-tone accents.
  2. Then darken the hue, not replace it. A 12–18% lightness reduction in OKLCH usually lifts a mid-tone accent past 4.5:1 while staying recognisable.
  3. Split the token. Keep the pure brand colour for large text and decorative fills; ship a darkened step as --brand-text for body-size labels and links.
  4. Do not rely on the eye. Every failure above was invisible to the person who shipped it. Measure the pair, not the swatch — you can do it in the contrast checker.
  5. Build the ramp up front. A ten-step scale from one hex gives you a compliant step for each state; the shades generator produces it in one click.

Method

  1. Fetched each homepage and up to three linked stylesheets on 2026-11-11.
  2. Counted every declared colour: hex, rgb()/rgba(), inline SVG fill, CSS colour keywords.
  3. Kept chromatic candidates (saturation ≥ 0.30, lightness 0.12–0.88) and took the most frequent as the accent.
  4. Weighted an explicit meta name="theme-color" six times — that tag is the site's own statement of its brand colour.
  5. Computed WCAG 2.2 contrast with the relative-luminance formula against white and black.

Limits. 26 of 96 sites were dropped: bot protection (HTTP 403/429), a page whose colour lives only in an image, or no chromatic declaration at all. This measures a declared CSS colour, not a rendered button, so treat it as a proxy for brand-colour legibility rather than a component-level audit. The sample is hand-picked well-known domains, not a traffic-weighted ranking.
