Accessible Color Palettes for Government & Healthcare Websites

Compliance-ready color schemes for healthcare and government sites that need to meet WCAG 2.2 AA. Includes ready-to-use palette examples.

By Yao Xiangqian · Nov 8, 2026 · 1800 words · 8 min read

Why Healthcare & Government Need Special Color Palettes

Healthcare and government websites serve the entire population — including older adults, people with visual impairments, and users with low digital literacy. A color palette that works fine for a startup's marketing site can be a real barrier for these users.

These sectors also often have legal requirements: Section 508 in the US, EN 301 549 in the EU, and various national accessibility laws all mandate WCAG 2.1 AA compliance at minimum. Many go further and require AAA for critical information.

But accessibility doesn't mean ugly. A well-designed accessible color palette can be both beautiful and inclusive.

Key Accessibility Requirements

Before we get to the palettes, let's recap the standards your colors need to meet:

StandardNormal TextLarge TextUI Components
WCAG 2.2 AA4.5:13:13:1
WCAG 2.2 AAA7:14.5:1—

Healthcare and government sites should aim for WCAG AA as the legal minimum and target AAA for critical user pathways (login, appointments, results, payments).

Also important but less discussed:

  • Color blindness: 8% of men have red-green color blindness. Never rely solely on color to convey meaning (error/success states, chart data).
  • Glare and halation: Pure white backgrounds can cause glare for users with cataracts. Slightly warm off-white backgrounds are easier on older eyes.
  • Focus indicators: Thick, high-contrast focus rings — not just a thin outline that blends in.
📋 Compliance tip: Document your color palette's contrast ratios in your design system. Include a table showing each color on each background with its ratio and pass/fail status. This makes audits much easier.

Colorblind-Safe Data Colors

Charts and data visualizations are especially tricky for healthcare and government. Avoid the standard red/green/blue/yellow rainbow palette — it's a disaster for colorblind users.

Use a colorblind-safe sequential or categorical palette instead:

The Okabe-Ito palette is especially recommended — it was developed specifically for maximum distinguishability for people with all types of color vision deficiency.

⚠️ Never rely on color alone: Even with a colorblind-safe palette, always pair color with another indicator — icons, patterns, shapes, labels, or tooltips. This is a WCAG requirement (1.4.1 Use of Color, level A).

Implementation Best Practices

Use design tokens, not hardcoded colors

Define your palette as semantic tokens — --color-primary, --color-error, --color-text-body — not just --blue-600. This makes it much easier to maintain dark mode, high-contrast mode, and theme updates.

Provide a high-contrast mode toggle

WCAG allows you to offer a high-contrast mode as an alternative, as long as it's easy to find and use. Many government sites include this as a standard feature.

Test with real users

Automated tools catch about 30% of accessibility issues. For the other 70%, you need real users — especially older adults and people with visual impairments. Include them in your testing process.

Audit regularly

Accessibility isn't a one-time check. New features, new components, and new content can all introduce new issues. Schedule quarterly audits and run automated checks in CI.
