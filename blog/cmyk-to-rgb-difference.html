CMYK to RGB: Why Colors Look Different on Screen vs Print

Understand the difference between additive and subtractive color, why printed colors never match screens, and how to convert CMYK to RGB correctly.

By Yao Xiangqian · Nov 8, 2026 · 1700 words · 8 min read
Advertisement

Why CMYK and RGB Are Different

Have you ever designed something on screen that looked perfect, then printed it and thought — "why does this look so dull and different?" You're not imagining it. Screen colors and printed colors are fundamentally different things, and understanding why will save you a lot of frustration.

The core difference comes down to additive vs subtractive color mixing — two completely different physical processes that produce color in opposite ways.

RGB: Additive Color (Light)

RGB (Red, Green, Blue) is how screens produce color. It's called "additive" because you start with black (no light) and add colored light on top of it.

  • Add all three colors at full intensity → white
  • No light at all → black
  • Different combinations make the 16.7 million colors we see on screen

Every screen — your phone, monitor, TV, even smartwatch — uses tiny red, green, and blue subpixels that emit light. When you look at a screen, you're looking directly at light sources.

CMYK: Subtractive Color (Ink)

CMYK (Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, Key/Black) is how printing produces color. It's called "subtractive" because you start with white (the paper) and subtract light by printing ink on top of it.

  • Ink absorbs (subtracts) certain wavelengths of light
  • The light that isn't absorbed bounces back to your eye — that's the color you see
  • Cyan ink absorbs red light, magenta absorbs green, yellow absorbs blue
  • Add all three and you should get black — but in practice you get a muddy brown, so printers add a pure black ink (the K in CMYK)

When you look at a printed page, you're looking at reflected ambient light — not a light source itself.

💡 Why K for black? Because B already means Blue in RGB, so the printing industry uses K for "Key plate" — the black plate that adds detail and contrast. It's also sometimes said to stand for the last letter of "blacK."

The Gamut Problem: Why They Never Match

"Gamut" is the range of colors a system can produce. RGB and CMYK have different gamuts — they overlap, but neither fits entirely inside the other.

Key differences:

  • RGB can produce vibrant colors CMYK can't: Bright, saturated colors — especially electric blues, neon greens, and vivid oranges — often look washed out when printed.
  • CMYK can produce some colors RGB can't: Certain deep blues, rich browns, and muted pastels fall outside sRGB but are achievable with ink.
  • Neither covers all visible colors: Both sRGB and CMYK are subsets of what the human eye can see.

How to Convert CMYK to RGB

There's no perfect formula because of the gamut differences and the many variables in printing (paper type, ink type, press quality). But there's a standard mathematical conversion that gives you a starting point:

The Standard Conversion Formula

Given C, M, Y, K values (each 0-1 or 0-100%):

  1. Convert percentages to 0-1 range (divide by 100)
  2. Calculate each RGB channel as: channel = 255 × (1 - c) × (1 - k)
  3. Where c is the CMY component for that channel (e.g., C affects R, M affects G, Y affects B)

More precisely:

R = 255 × (1 - C/100) × (1 - K/100)

G = 255 × (1 - M/100) × (1 - K/100)

B = 255 × (1 - Y/100) × (1 - K/100)

Example: Converting a Deep Blue

CMYK: C=100, M=80, Y=0, K=30

ChannelCalculationValue
Red255 × (1 - 1) × (1 - 0.3) = 255 × 0 × 0.70
Green255 × (1 - 0.8) × (1 - 0.3) = 255 × 0.2 × 0.7≈ 36
Blue255 × (1 - 0) × (1 - 0.3) = 255 × 1 × 0.7≈ 179

Result: rgb(0, 36, 179) or #0024B3

But remember — this is just a mathematical approximation. The actual printed color will look different depending on the printer, paper, and viewing conditions.

Why Your Prints Look Dull (And How to Fix It)

The #1 complaint from designers: "my prints look nothing like my screen." Here are the most common reasons and what to do about them:

1. Your screen is too bright

Most monitors are set to 100% brightness out of the box, which is way too bright for design work. A properly calibrated monitor should be at 120 cd/m² — roughly the brightness of paper under office lighting.

2. You're using sRGB for print

sRGB is the standard web color space and it's smaller than print gamut in some areas but larger in others. For print work, design in Adobe RGB or the specific CMYK profile of your printer (SWOP, FOGRA, etc.).

3. No color management

Design software like Photoshop, Figma, and InDesign support color profiles. Use them. Set your document's color profile to match your printer's output profile — don't just trust the RGB preview.

4. Paper matters more than you think

The same CMYK values look completely different on glossy photo paper vs matte vs newsprint. Glossy paper gives deeper blacks and more saturated colors. Newsprint absorbs ink and makes everything look muted.

⚠️ Proof before you print: Always ask for a printed proof before a large print run. Digital soft proofs (on-screen CMYK previews) help, but nothing beats seeing the actual ink on actual paper.

Spot Colors: When CMYK Isn't Enough

Sometimes you need an exact color that CMYK can't reproduce — like a specific brand red or a metallic gold. That's where spot colors (like Pantone colors) come in.

Spot colors are pre-mixed inks that print as a single color, rather than being mixed from CMYK halftone dots. They're more expensive (each spot color is a separate plate on the press) but give you exact, consistent color across different print runs and materials.

Most brands specify their primary logo color as a Pantone spot color, then provide CMYK and RGB approximations for other uses.
